<Section id="why" heading="Why fit an energy–volume curve">

An E–V curve gives the equilibrium volume and the bulk modulus without using the stress tensor. So it
is free of Pulay stress at a fixed cutoff<Cite id="S-042" />. It is also how you compare polymorphs or
magnetic states over a range of volumes, and how you get transition pressures.

The idea: you choose the volume instead of letting VASP change it. You make several copies of the
relaxed cell, each scaled a little, relax everything except the volume, and record the final energy
and volume of each. The points trace a bowl, and a fitted curve gives the bottom of the bowl (V0, E0)
and how steep it is (B0).

</Section>

<Section id="inputs" heading="The inputs">

Each point is a fixed-volume relaxation. Use one INCAR for all of them, starting from your
[relaxation](/workflow/relaxation/) INCAR and changing these tags<Cite id="S-042" />:

| Tag            | Setting                 | Reason                                                                                                                 |
| -------------- | ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| ISIF           | 4 (2 for cubic)         | 4 relaxes the ions and the cell shape at fixed volume. A cubic cell has no shape to relax, so 2 (ions only) is enough. |
| IBRION         | 2                       | Relax the ions (and shape) at each volume.                                                                             |
| NSW            | 100                     | Up to 100 ionic steps per point.                                                                                       |
| EDIFF          | 1E-6                    | The SCF criterion at every point.                                                                                      |
| EDIFFG         | −0.005                  | Force criterion, as in the relaxation.                                                                                 |
| ENCUT, KPOINTS | The same at every point | The points are compared with each other, so nothing else may change between them.                                      |

Scan 7 to 11 volumes covering about ±5 to 10% in volume.

<Section id="run" heading="Run the volume scan">

The author's script `ev_scan.sh` scales the lattice constant (line 2 of POSCAR) from 0.97 to 1.03, which
is roughly ±9% in volume. For each scale it makes a folder `s_0.97`, `s_0.98`, and so on, copies the
inputs in, runs VASP, and appends the final volume and the "energy without entropy" to `ev.dat`:

<VaspFile
  name="shell"
  code={`#!/bin/bash
# INCAR: ISIF = 4 (shape + ions relax at fixed volume; use ISIF = 2 for cubic), IBRION = 2,
#        NSW = 100, EDIFF = 1E-6, EDIFFG = -0.005, same ENCUT and k-mesh everywhere
for s in 0.97 0.98 0.99 1.00 1.01 1.02 1.03; do        # lattice scale → ≈ ±9% volume
  d=s_$s; mkdir -p $d; cp INCAR KPOINTS POTCAR $d/
  awk -v s=$s 'NR==2{print $1*s; next}1' POSCAR > $d/POSCAR
  (cd $d && mpirun -np 32 vasp_std > vasp.out)
  V=$(grep "volume of cell" $d/OUTCAR | tail -1 | awk '{print $NF}')
  E=$(grep "energy  without entropy" $d/OUTCAR | tail -1 | awk '{print $NF}')
  echo "$V $E" >> ev.dat
done`}
/>

The `awk` line multiplies the scale factor on line 2 of POSCAR, so the whole cell grows or shrinks
evenly. `ev.dat` ends up with one line per point: volume in Å³, energy in eV per cell.

</Section>

<Section id="fit" heading="Fit the Birch–Murnaghan curve (3rd order)">

The third-order Birch–Murnaghan equation of state<Cite id="S-061" /><Cite id="S-062" />, as the author
writes it<Cite id="S-042" />:

- `E(V) = E₀ + (9V₀B₀/16) { [(V₀/V)^(2/3) − 1]³ B′₀ + [(V₀/V)^(2/3) − 1]² [6 − 4(V₀/V)^(2/3)] }`
- `B₀ [GPa] = B₀ [eV/Å³] × 160.2177`

E₀ is the minimum energy, V₀ the equilibrium volume, B₀ the bulk modulus and B′₀ its pressure
derivative. With E in eV and V in Å³ the fit gives B₀ in eV/Å³, so multiply by 160.2177 for GPa.

Save this script as `fit_eos.py` next to `ev.dat` and run it with Python. It fits a parabola first to get
starting guesses, then fits the Birch–Murnaghan curve:

<VaspFile
  name="shell"
  code={`import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import curve_fit
V, E = np.loadtxt("ev.dat", unpack=True)          # Å^3, eV (per cell)
def bm3(V, E0, V0, B0, Bp):
    x = (V0 / V) ** (2.0 / 3.0)
    return E0 + 9.0 * V0 * B0 / 16.0 * ((x - 1) ** 3 * Bp + (x - 1) ** 2 * (6 - 4 * x))
a, b, c = np.polyfit(V, E, 2)                     # parabola for initial guesses
V0g = -b / (2 * a)
p, _ = curve_fit(bm3, V, E, p0=[E.min(), V0g, 2 * a * V0g, 4.0])
E0, V0, B0, Bp = p
print(f"E0 = {E0:.5f} eV  V0 = {V0:.3f} A^3  B0 = {B0 * 160.2177:.1f} GPa  B' = {Bp:.2f}")`}
/>

Other ways to fit the same data<Cite id="S-042" />:

- **VASPkit 205** fits the equation of state directly and prints E0, V0, B0 and B′<Cite id="S-020" />.
- **pymatgen:** `EOS(eos_name="birch_murnaghan")`<Cite id="S-034" />.
- **ASE:** its `EquationOfState` class.
- **This site:** paste `ev.dat` into the [Equation of state fit](/tools/eos/) tool.

</Section>

<Section id="check" heading="Check before you move on">

- **The minimum lies inside the scanned range.** If the lowest point is at an edge, extend the scan
  to smaller or larger scales.
- **B′0 is usually 3.5 to 5.** Values far outside suggest too few points, too narrow a range or noisy
  energies.
- **B0 agrees with the elastic constants.** B_H from the Cij of the [elastic step](/workflow/elastic/)
  should match B0 within a few percent. A large difference points to an unconverged ENCUT or k-mesh, or
  to residual stress<Cite id="S-042" />.

</Section>

<Section id="polymorphs" heading="Polymorphs and pressure">

To compare phases, run the scan for each polymorph (or each magnetic state) and plot all the E(V)
curves together. The common tangent between two curves gives the transition pressure. Equivalently,
compare the enthalpies `H = E + PV`, with the pressure from `P = −dE/dV`<Cite id="S-042" />.

</Section>

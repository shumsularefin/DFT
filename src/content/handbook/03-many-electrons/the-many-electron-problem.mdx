<Why>

In module 2 you solved the hydrogen atom exactly. A solid has something like $10^{23}$ electrons. You might hope to repeat the same steps with a bigger computer. This page explains why that hope fails, and why the failure is not about computer speed.

</Why>

<Intuition>

Hydrogen is easy because there is one electron. It only feels the nucleus. Add a second electron and a new thing appears: the two electrons repel each other, so where one goes depends on where the other is. Their motions are tied together. This is the <Term id="many-body-problem">many-body problem</Term> <Cite id="S-048" />.

Here is the central difficulty, in plain words. For one electron, the wavefunction is a number at every point in 3D space. For two electrons, it is a number for every _pair_ of points, which is six coordinates. For $N$ electrons it needs $3N$ coordinates. You cannot split it into $N$ separate pictures, because the electrons are linked.

A quick count shows the scale. Suppose you note the wavefunction on a grid of 10 points along each direction. One electron needs $10^3$ numbers. Ten electrons need $10^{30}$. This is arithmetic, not a model: ten electrons, thirty coordinates, ten values each.

We make one simplification right away: treat the heavy nuclei as fixed while the light electrons move. This is the <Term id="born-oppenheimer-approximation">Born-Oppenheimer approximation</Term> <Cite id="S-048" />. It is usually very good, and it is what lets us talk about "the electrons in a fixed arrangement of atoms".

<Level min="intermediate">

The wavefunction also has to change sign when two electrons swap places, which is the Pauli principle from module 2. Putting this into a many-electron state is why <Term id="slater-determinant">Slater determinants</Term> exist; the next page counts them.

</Level>

<Level min="pro">

The electronic <Term id="hamiltonian">Hamiltonian</Term> for fixed nuclei has three parts: the kinetic energy of each electron, the attraction of each electron to the nuclei, and the repulsion between every pair of electrons <Cite id="S-048" />. Without the last term the problem would split into $N$ one-electron problems and be easy. That single term, which couples all pairs, is the entire difficulty.

</Level>

</Intuition>

<TheMath>

For electrons at positions $\mathbf{r}_1, \dots, \mathbf{r}_N$ in the field of fixed nuclei, in atomic-style notation <Cite id="S-048" />,

$$\hat H = \sum_{i}\left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla_i^2 + v(\mathbf{r}_i)\right] + \sum_{i<j}\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,|\mathbf{r}_i-\mathbf{r}_j|}$$

where $v$ is the attraction to the nuclei. The first sum treats each electron on its own. The second sum couples every pair, and is the part that makes the wavefunction a function of all $3N$ coordinates at once. A grid with $g$ points per axis needs $g^{3N}$ values, which is $10^{30}$ for $g = 10$ and $N = 10$.

</TheMath>

<InVasp>

VASP does not attempt the exact many-electron wavefunction. Instead of $3N$ coordinates it works with the electron density, which depends on three, as the next pages explain. It also treats only the valence electrons explicitly; the core electrons are folded into the POTCAR files you supply.

</InVasp>

<WhatChanges>

| If you change...              | ...the difficulty                    | ...why                                        |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------- |
| Add one electron              | Multiplies the size of the problem   | Three more coordinates in the wavefunction    |
| Switch off electron repulsion | Becomes easy                         | The problem splits into one-electron problems |
| Freeze the nuclei             | Removes nuclear motion from the task | Born-Oppenheimer                              |

Why the repulsion matters: it is real and large. What happens if you drop it: you compute a much simpler problem whose answers are not those of the material.

</WhatChanges>

<Mistakes>

<Callout variant="mistake" title="Blaming the difficulty on computer speed">

Faster computers help, but the size of the exact problem grows multiplicatively with each added electron. Waiting for hardware does not close that gap. The next page shows the numbers.

</Callout>

<Callout variant="mistake" title="Thinking each electron has its own wavefunction">

Orbitals are a useful picture, but the exact state belongs to all the electrons together.

</Callout>

</Mistakes>

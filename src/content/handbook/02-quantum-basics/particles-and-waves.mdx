<Why>

Every later idea in this course, from energy levels to bands to the plane waves VASP uses, rests on one surprising fact: at the scale of atoms, electrons behave like waves. If you carry the picture of an electron as a tiny ball orbiting a nucleus, the rest of the course will not make sense. This page replaces that picture with a better one.

</Why>

<Intuition>

Start with something familiar. A <Term id="wave">wave</Term> on a rope is a pattern that repeats along the rope. Three numbers describe it: how tall it is (<Term id="amplitude">amplitude</Term>), how many times per second it repeats (<Term id="frequency">frequency</Term>), and how far apart the peaks are (the wavelength).

Now the surprise. Light, which we usually think of as a wave, also arrives in lumps called <Term id="photon">photons</Term>. And electrons, which we usually think of as particles, also show wave patterns: fired at a thin crystal, they scatter into the same kind of ring pattern that waves make <Cite id="S-047" />. This is called <Term id="wave-particle-duality">wave-particle duality</Term>.

A helpful way to hold both ideas: an electron is neither a ball nor a ripple. It is a third kind of thing that sometimes looks like one and sometimes like the other. When it is squeezed into a small space, the wave side dominates. That is exactly the situation of an electron in a material.

<Level min="intermediate">

The wavelength of a particle is set by its momentum. This is the <Term id="de-broglie-wavelength">de Broglie wavelength</Term>, $\lambda = h/p$, where $h$ is the <Term id="planck-constant">Planck constant</Term> <Cite id="S-047" />. A faster or heavier particle has a shorter wavelength, which is why a thrown ball shows no wave behavior at all while an electron does.

</Level>

<Level min="pro">

For a free electron with kinetic energy $E$, $p^2 = 2mE$, so $\lambda = 2\pi/\sqrt{E/(\hbar^2/2m)}$. With $\hbar^2/2m = 3.80998\ \text{eV Å}^2$ <Cite id="S-031" />, an electron with 100 eV of kinetic energy has a wavelength of about 1.23 Å, which is comparable to the spacing between atoms in a crystal. That match is why electron diffraction works, and why electrons in solids cannot be treated as classical points.

</Level>

</Intuition>

<TheMath>

The de Broglie relation links the two descriptions: $\lambda = h/p$, and equivalently the wave number $k = 2\pi/\lambda = p/\hbar$ <Cite id="S-047" />. The kinetic energy of a free particle is then

$$E = \frac{p^2}{2m} = \frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2m}$$

The combination $\hbar^2/2m$ appears again and again in this course. In the units used by VASP and by this site it is $3.80998\ \text{eV Å}^2$ <Cite id="S-031" />. The site computes it from the fundamental constants and checks the result against that value in a unit test.

</TheMath>

<InVasp>

VASP does not store electrons as little balls. It describes them as waves, built from plane waves, which you will meet in module 5. The idea that an electron is a wave with a wavelength is already enough to see why a quantity such as the cutoff energy (`ENCUT`) exists: it limits how short a wavelength the program is allowed to use.

</InVasp>

<WhatChanges>

| If you change...            | ...the wavelength | ...what you see                              |
| --------------------------- | ----------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| The electron's energy up    | Gets shorter      | Finer wave pattern, more detail, more cost   |
| The particle's mass up      | Gets shorter      | Wave behavior becomes harder to notice       |
| The space it is squeezed in | Not directly      | Only waves that fit are allowed (next pages) |

Why this matters: the wave picture is what makes energies come in steps. What happens if you ignore it: you predict that atoms are unstable and that materials have no electronic structure, which is wrong.

</WhatChanges>

<Mistakes>

<Callout variant="mistake" title="Picturing the electron as a ball orbiting the nucleus">

That picture is a historical stepping stone. The electron in an atom is a standing wave spread around the nucleus, not a point moving along a track.

</Callout>

<Callout variant="mistake" title="Thinking the wave is made of many electrons">

One electron is one wave. The wave describes where that single electron may be found, which is the subject of the next page.

</Callout>

</Mistakes>

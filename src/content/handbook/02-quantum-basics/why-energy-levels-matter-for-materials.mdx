<Why>

Whether a material is a metal, a semiconductor or an insulator depends on what energies its electrons are allowed to have and which of those are filled. This page connects the single-atom ideas of this module to that bigger picture, and tells you where the course goes next.

</Why>

<Intuition>

One atom has separate energy levels, like the steps in the box. Bring two atoms close and each level splits into two. Bring many atoms together, as in a crystal, and each level spreads into a crowd of closely spaced levels that looks like a continuous stripe. That stripe is a <Term id="band">band</Term> <Cite id="S-050" />.

Between bands there may be a range of energies with no allowed states: a <Term id="band-gap">band gap</Term>. Electrons fill levels from the bottom, two per level, as the last page described. If the top filled band is completely full and a gap lies above it, small pushes cannot move electrons, and the material is an insulator or a semiconductor. If a band is partly filled, electrons move easily, and the material is a metal <Cite id="S-036" />.

How many states sit at each energy is called the <Term id="density-of-states">density of states</Term>. You will meet bands, the gap and the density of states in modules 4 and 5, and they are among the main things a DFT calculation reports.

<Level min="intermediate">

The size of the gap sets whether a semiconductor absorbs visible light, and which dopants are useful. This is why predicting a gap correctly is a standard test of a method, and why the next modules spend so much time on approximations.

</Level>

<Level min="pro">

The unit of energy used for all this is the <Term id="electron-volt">electron volt</Term>, and lengths in the crystal are given in <Term id="angstrom">ångström</Term>. Band gaps of materials range from a fraction of an electron volt to several electron volts. Common DFT approximations are known to underestimate gaps <Cite id="S-049" />, a point that comes back later.

</Level>

</Intuition>

<TheMath>

No new formula is needed for this page. The key relation is the one from the box: confinement sets $E_n \propto n^2/L^2$ <Cite id="S-047" />. In a crystal the allowed waves are labeled by a wave number $k$, and the energy of an electron as a function of $k$ draws out the bands <Cite id="S-050" />. The simplest case, a free electron, is $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$ from the first page of this module.

</TheMath>

<InVasp>

VASP reports the energies of electrons at many $k$ points, and the tools around it turn those into band structures and a density of states. The occupation of levels is controlled by settings you meet later, such as `ISMEAR` and `SIGMA`. The picture from this page is how to read their output.

</InVasp>

<WhatChanges>

| If you change...      | ...the bands                  | ...the material               |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Atoms closer together | Bands get wider               | Easier electron movement      |
| Number of electrons   | Where the filling stops moves | Could turn metal to insulator |
| Atom type             | Level positions change        | Different gap                 |

Why this matters: the band picture lets us answer practical questions about materials without following every electron.

</WhatChanges>

<Mistakes>

<Callout variant="mistake" title="Thinking a band is a single orbit">

A band is a huge number of closely spaced levels spread over the whole crystal, not one state.

</Callout>

<Callout variant="mistake" title="Treating this page as the full theory of solids">

It is a sketch to give you the vocabulary. The proper treatment comes in later modules.

</Callout>

</Mistakes>

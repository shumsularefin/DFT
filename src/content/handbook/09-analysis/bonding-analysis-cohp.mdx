<Why>

A density of states tells you where the electron levels are. It does not tell you whether filling a level holds two atoms together or pushes them apart. That is the chemist's question: is this bond strong, are antibonding states filled, why does adding an electron weaken the structure? The <Term id="cohp">crystal orbital Hamilton population</Term> (COHP) answers it by splitting the density of states of an atom pair into bonding and antibonding parts <Cite id="S-128" />. Plane waves have no atoms in them, so the program LOBSTER first projects the VASP wavefunctions onto atomic-like orbitals <Cite id="S-129" />.

</Why>

<Intuition>

Two orbitals on neighbouring atoms combine into a lower, bonding combination and a higher, antibonding one. Filling the bonding one lowers the energy; filling the antibonding one raises it. COHP weights each state at energy $E$ by how much it lowers or raises the energy of one particular pair of atoms.

**The sign convention.** A bonding contribution lowers the energy, so its COHP is _negative_. Plots therefore show **−COHP**: to the right of zero (positive) is bonding, to the left (negative) is antibonding <Cite id="S-128" /> <Cite id="S-042" />. Look at where the Fermi level sits: antibonding states filled just below it signal a weakened or strained bond, and often an instability.

**Integrated COHP.** Integrating the COHP up to the Fermi level gives the <Term id="icohp">ICOHP</Term>, one number per pair. A more negative ICOHP means a stronger covalent interaction. It is a relative measure: compare similar bonds computed with the same basis and settings, not raw values across very different systems <Cite id="S-129" />.

**COOP versus COHP.** The older crystal orbital overlap population (COOP) weights the states by the overlap of the two orbitals instead of their Hamiltonian matrix element. In COOP bonding is positive <Cite id="S-128" />. COHP is the natural choice with DFT because the Hamiltonian element carries an energy, so its integral relates to the band energy.

**Projection quality.** LOBSTER reconstructs the density from its local basis. The fraction it fails to reproduce is the <Term id="charge-spilling">charge spilling</Term>, printed in `lobsterout`. A small spilling means the basis describes the states well; a large one means part of the bonding is missing from the picture <Cite id="S-129" />.

</Intuition>

<TheMath>

For orbital $\mu$ on atom A and orbital $\nu$ on atom B, with Hamiltonian matrix element $H_{\mu\nu}$, band $j$, k-point $\mathbf{k}$, occupation $f_{j\mathbf{k}}$ and expansion coefficients $c$ of the band in the local basis <Cite id="S-128" />:

$$
\text{COHP}_{\mu\nu}(E) = H_{\mu\nu}\sum_{j,\mathbf{k}} f_{j\mathbf{k}}\,c^{*}_{\mu,j\mathbf{k}}\,c_{\nu,j\mathbf{k}}\,\delta(E - \varepsilon_{j\mathbf{k}})
$$

Summing over the orbitals of A and B gives the pair's COHP. COOP replaces $H_{\mu\nu}$ by the overlap $S_{\mu\nu}$. The integral is

$$
\text{ICOHP} = \int_{-\infty}^{E_F} \text{COHP}(E)\,dE
$$

<Level min="intermediate">

In a plane-wave code the coefficients $c$ do not exist directly. LOBSTER projects the PAW wavefunctions onto a minimal basis of atomic-like orbitals fitted for that purpose (for example the `pbeVaspFit2015` set), and computes the "projected COHP" from the projected Hamiltonian <Cite id="S-129" />. The charge spilling measures how much of the occupied density is lost in that projection.

</Level>

<Level min="pro">

The COHP depends on the basis, so ICOHP values are not observables and are not unique. They are useful as trends within one study <Cite id="S-129" />. Newer LOBSTER versions add further bond indicators: pymatgen's interface, for example, has switches for crystal orbital bond indices (COBI) marked as LOBSTER 4.1.0 features <Cite id="S-034" />. Their definitions are in the LOBSTER documentation, which this page does not cover.

</Level>

</TheMath>

<InVasp>

LOBSTER runs after a VASP static calculation and reads its `WAVECAR`. The VASP run needs <Cite id="S-129" /> <Cite id="S-034" />:

- **No ionic steps:** `NSW = 0` on the relaxed structure.
- **Symmetry off:** `ISYM = 0` (time-reversal symmetry kept) or `ISYM = -1` (none). LOBSTER needs the k-points the symmetry would otherwise fold away. pymatgen's LOBSTER input writer accepts only these two values; check the manual of your LOBSTER version for which it supports.
- **Wavefunctions written:** `LWAVE = .TRUE.`
- **Enough bands:** `NBANDS` at least the number of local basis functions in the cell (each s orbital counts 1, p 3, d 5, f 7, per atom). pymatgen sets it to exactly that number.
- **PAW potentials only.** LOBSTER works with PAW, which is what VASP `POTCAR`s are; other potential types are rejected.
- A uniform k-mesh, as for a DOS run.

For diamond-structure Si (two atoms, basis 3s 3p, so 2 x 4 = 8 basis functions):

<VaspFile
  name="INCAR"
  code={`SYSTEM = Si for LOBSTER
NSW    = 0
ISYM   = 0
LWAVE  = .TRUE.
NBANDS = 8
ISMEAR = 0
SIGMA  = 0.05
ENCUT  = 520
EDIFF  = 1E-6`}
  annotations={{
    2: 'static run on the relaxed structure',
    3: 'time-reversal symmetry only',
    4: 'LOBSTER reads the WAVECAR',
    5: 'two Si atoms times (one s + three p) basis functions',
    6: 'Gaussian smearing; give LOBSTER the same width',
  }}
/>

And a matching `lobsterin` (keywords as pymatgen writes them <Cite id="S-034" />):

```text
COHPstartEnergy   -15.0
COHPendEnergy       5.0
basisSet          pbeVaspFit2015
basisfunctions    Si 3s 3p
gaussianSmearingWidth 0.05
cohpGenerator     from 0.1 to 3.0
```

The energy window (relative to the Fermi level) and the 3.0 Å distance range are illustrative; choose them to cover the bands and pairs you care about. LOBSTER writes `COHPCAR.lobster` (the curves), `ICOHPLIST.lobster` (one ICOHP per pair) and `lobsterout`, where you read the charge spilling first.

</InVasp>

<WhatChanges>

| If you change...              | ...what happens                                                              |
| ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `ISYM` left at its default    | LOBSTER cannot use the symmetry-reduced k-points; the run stops or is wrong  |
| `NBANDS` below the basis size | The projection has fewer bands than orbitals; LOBSTER refuses or misprojects |
| A smaller basis               | Larger charge spilling; COHP misses part of the bonding                      |
| Plot COHP instead of −COHP    | Bonding and antibonding swap sides; easy to misread                          |

These rows follow from the requirements above; they are reasoning, not quoted values.

Why COHP: it says which states bond, not just where they are. Why these settings: LOBSTER needs every k-point and enough bands to project. What happens if you skip the spilling check: a confident bonding story from a projection that missed part of the density.

</WhatChanges>

<Mistakes>

<Callout variant="mistake" title="Reading the sign the wrong way round">

COHP is negative for bonding; −COHP is positive for bonding. Check which one your plotting tool shows before calling a peak antibonding <Cite id="S-128" />.

</Callout>

<Callout variant="mistake" title="Running LOBSTER on a symmetry-reduced run">

A standard SCF uses `ISYM` symmetry and writes only the irreducible k-points. Redo the static run with `ISYM = 0` or `-1` and `LWAVE = .TRUE.` <Cite id="S-034" />.

</Callout>

<Callout variant="mistake" title="Too few bands">

The default `NBANDS` can be smaller than the number of basis functions, especially with d or f orbitals in the basis. Count them and set `NBANDS` explicitly.

</Callout>

<Callout variant="mistake" title="Comparing ICOHP across different bases">

ICOHP depends on the basis and settings. Compare bonds within one consistent set of calculations, not with a number from another paper <Cite id="S-129" />.

</Callout>

</Mistakes>

<Why>

A worked example is only useful if the system is real enough to go wrong in interesting ways. The rare-earth cuprates $R\mathrm{CuO}_3$ (with $R$ a trivalent rare earth such as La, Nd or Sm) are a good choice for three reasons. They are <Term id="perovskite">perovskites</Term>, the structure family that the tools on this site are built around. Their size trend across the lanthanide row is a clean test of the <Term id="tolerance-factor">tolerance-factor</Term> ideas. And they contain copper in the rare $+3$ state, which forces every decision that makes transition-metal oxides hard in DFT: spin, magnetism, a possible Hubbard $U$ and strong copper-oxygen mixing.

This page decides which member of the family to calculate first and what to expect before any VASP run. Nothing on it is a result of the author's calculations.

</Why>

<Intuition>

This worked example documents the complete `RCuO3` workflow and input protocol. Computed DFT result numbers will be added after the research is published. Until then this page shows the method and the inputs only; no result on it is a real or invented number.

</Callout>

**The structure in one picture.** In an $AB\mathrm{O}_3$ perovskite, each small $B$ cation (here Cu) sits at the centre of an octahedron of six oxygen atoms. The octahedra share corners and form a three-dimensional framework. The large $A$ cation (here the rare earth $R$) sits in the cavity between eight octahedra, where it has twelve oxygen neighbours in the ideal cubic structure <Cite id="S-002" />. The ideal cubic cell holds one formula unit: one $R$, one Cu and three O, five atoms in all.

**Why the size of $R$ matters.** If the $A$ cation is a little too small for its cavity, the framework does not stay straight. The octahedra rotate together to close the gap around $A$, a cooperative <Term id="octahedral-tilting">octahedral tilting</Term> <Cite id="S-101" />. Tilting lowers the symmetry and makes the repeating cell two or four times larger. Across the lanthanide row the $R^{3+}$ ion shrinks steadily (the lanthanide contraction), so the family runs from nearly cubic towards strongly tilted while the copper site stays chemically the same.

**Why trivalent copper is unusual.** Copper in oxides is almost always $+1$ or $+2$. In the oxidation-state counts that the site's checks use, copper carries $+3$ in 325 entries, about 3 percent of the copper entries with a non-zero state <Cite id="S-051" />. The tolerance-factor explorer therefore flags $\mathrm{Cu}^{3+}$ as "known but uncommon". Chemically, $\mathrm{Cu}^{3+}$ is $3d^8$: removing three electrons from $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^{10}4s^1$ leaves eight $d$ electrons <Cite id="S-100" />. Such a highly charged copper ion pulls electrons from its oxygen neighbours so strongly that the holes sit partly on oxygen rather than purely on copper. In the language of the Zaanen-Sawatzky-Allen scheme the <Term id="charge-transfer-energy">charge-transfer energy</Term> is small or even negative <Cite id="S-098" />, as photoemission found for the formally $\mathrm{Cu}^{3+}$ compound $\mathrm{NaCuO}_2$ <Cite id="S-099" />. For a DFT calculation this means strong Cu 3d and O 2p mixing, which is exactly where semilocal functionals and DFT+U need care.

**What the literature says (literature, not this project).** $\mathrm{LaCuO}_3$, the largest-$R$ member, was first made under high oxygen pressure <Cite id="S-097" />. Later work reported the fully oxidized compound as rhombohedral, oxygen-deficient forms with lower symmetry, and metallic behaviour with a weak, nearly temperature-independent magnetic susceptibility <Cite id="S-096" />. That gives the calculation a qualitative target: a model that predicts a wide-gap magnetic insulator for fully oxidized $\mathrm{LaCuO}_3$ disagrees with these measurements, and the disagreement must be explained, not ignored.

**The choice.** The worked example starts with $\mathrm{LaCuO}_3$: it has the largest tolerance factor in the family, a measured structure to compare against, and the plainest rare-earth potential (lanthanum has an empty $4f$ shell). Smaller $R$ come later, once the protocol works.

</Intuition>

<TheMath>

The <Term id="ionic-radius">ionic radii</Term> $r_A$, $r_B$ and $r_X$ are Shannon's effective ionic radii, looked up for the right oxidation state and coordination number <Cite id="S-001" />. Goldschmidt's tolerance factor compares two ways of measuring the ideal cubic cell edge: along the $B$-O-$B$ line, $a = 2(r_B + r_X)$, and along the face diagonal through $A$, $a\sqrt{2} = 2(r_A + r_X)$ <Cite id="S-002" />. Their ratio is

$$t = \frac{r_A + r_X}{\sqrt{2}\,(r_B + r_X)} .$$

At $t = 1$ both fit exactly. Below 1 the $A$ cation is too small and the octahedra tilt. The octahedral factor $\mu = r_B / r_X$ checks the $B$ site on its own: by the radius-ratio rule a $B\mathrm{X}_6$ octahedron is geometrically comfortable for $\sqrt{2} - 1 \le \mu \le \sqrt{3} - 1$ <Cite id="S-022" />. Bartel and co-workers proposed a second descriptor that uses the oxidation state $n_A$ of the $A$ cation <Cite id="S-003" />:

$$\tau = \frac{r_X}{r_B} - n_A\left(n_A - \frac{r_A/r_B}{\ln(r_A/r_B)}\right), \qquad \tau < 4.18 \text{ suggests a perovskite.}$$

**What the site's library computes for RCuO3.** With $\mathrm{Cu}^{3+}$ six-coordinated (Shannon lists it with the label LS, 0.54 Å) and $\mathrm{O}^{2-}$ six-coordinated (1.40 Å), the octahedral factor is $\mu = 0.54/1.40 \approx 0.386$, below $\sqrt 2 - 1 \approx 0.414$ <Cite id="S-001" />. The explorer therefore warns that Cu looks too small for its octahedron by the radius-ratio rule. Real $\mathrm{Cu}^{3+}$ perovskites exist <Cite id="S-096" />, so this is a reminder that the rule is a heuristic, not a verdict.

Shannon lists twelve-coordinated radii for only a few trivalent rare earths, so the $A$ site needs an explicit coordination policy. The table below is the output of the explorer's "Lanthanide sweep" (Cu as $B$, O as $X$) under three policies. These are numbers from the site's own code applied to Shannon's radii, not results of a DFT calculation and not measurements.

<Level min="intermediate">

Three things to read from it. First, the trend is the same under every policy: $t$ falls steadily from La to Lu, so tilting should grow along the series. Second, the absolute values depend on the policy by about 0.05, which is larger than the differences between neighbouring rare earths, so never compare a CN 12 value with a CN 9 value. Third, every $\tau$ is well below 4.18, so the descriptor does not rule out any member. Bartel and co-workers take the $A$ radius at the coordination closest to 12, so the strict CN 12 column follows their convention and the other columns do not <Cite id="S-003" />.

</Level>

<Level min="pro">

The descriptors are geometric. They know nothing about the competing phases (other copper oxides, rare-earth cuprates with copper in the $+2$ state, and oxygen gas) that decide whether $R\mathrm{CuO}_3$ is stable, and the need for high oxygen pressure in the synthesis <Cite id="S-097" /> says that the oxygen chemical potential matters. A convex-hull analysis with an oxygen reference is the DFT test that the tolerance factor cannot replace.

</Level>

</TheMath>

<InVasp>

Nothing here is a VASP run yet; this is the screening step that comes before one. Step by step:

1. Open the <a href="/tools/tolerance-factor/">tolerance-factor explorer</a>. Set $A$ to La (+3, CN 12), $B$ to Cu (+3, CN 6, spin LS), $X$ to O (−2, CN 6). Read $t$, $\mu$ and $\tau$ and every warning in the list: the octahedral-factor warning and the uncommon-oxidation-state warning both appear.
2. Open "Maps, family trend and exports", choose "Lanthanide sweep", and switch the coordination policy between strict CN XII, uniform CN IX and uniform CN VIII. Export the CSV for your notes.
3. Record in your notebook the oxidation states, coordination numbers and policy you used <Cite id="S-001" />. Without them nobody can reproduce your $t$.
4. Pick the first compound. Here it is $\mathrm{LaCuO}_3$, for the reasons above. The next page builds its input files.

The magnetism question (does $3d^8$ copper carry a moment here?) cannot be answered by screening. It is the first thing the VASP runs have to settle, and the next page explains how.

</InVasp>

<WhatChanges>

| If you change...                         | ...then the descriptors                        | ...and the DFT setup                                              |
| ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| $R$ from La towards Lu                   | $t$ falls; tilting is expected to grow         | Larger cells (10 or 20 atoms) are needed to hold the tilts        |
| The A-site coordination policy           | Every $t$ shifts by a few hundredths           | Nothing in VASP; only comparisons across policies break           |
| $\mathrm{Cu}^{3+}$ to $\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}$ | $\mu$ grows; charge balance fails for $R^{3+}$ | A different compound with less oxygen                             |
| La to a rare earth with $4f$ electrons   | Radius changes only                            | A different PAW choice ($4f$ in the core) and more care with spin |

Why screen first: the descriptors cost nothing and tell you which cell sizes and symmetries to prepare. Why this value of $t$ matters: it sets whether a 5-atom cubic cell is a reasonable start. What happens if you skip it: you may relax a structure in a cell too small to hold the distortion it wants.

</WhatChanges>

<Mistakes>

<Callout variant="mistake" title="Treating a tolerance factor as proof of stability">

A $t$ near 1 or a $\tau$ below 4.18 says the ions fit together, not that the compound is stable against its competing oxides. Fix: treat the descriptors as a filter and test stability with DFT energies and a convex hull.

</Callout>

<Callout variant="mistake" title="Mixing coordination policies in one comparison">

Taking a CN 12 radius for La and a CN 9 radius for Gd because CN 12 is missing makes Gd look far more tilted than it is. Fix: use one policy for the whole series and state it, as the explorer's sweep does.

</Callout>

<Callout variant="mistake" title="Ignoring the warnings because the compound exists">

The octahedral-factor warning for $\mathrm{Cu}^{3+}$ is not a reason to stop, but it is a reason to expect short, strained Cu-O bonds and strong covalency. Fix: write the warning into your notes and check the Cu-O distances after relaxation.

</Callout>

<Callout variant="mistake" title="Assuming the formal d8 count tells you the spin state">

The formal count says nothing about how much of the hole sits on oxygen, or whether the compound is metallic. Fix: compute non-magnetic and magnetic starts and compare their energies, as the next pages do.

</Callout>

</Mistakes>

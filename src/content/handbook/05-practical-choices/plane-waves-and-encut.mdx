<Why>

The Kohn-Sham equations need orbitals, and a computer cannot store a smooth function exactly. It stores a list of numbers: the amounts of some set of building-block functions. VASP builds orbitals from <Term id="plane-wave">plane waves</Term> and `ENCUT` decides how many. Too few and the result is wrong; too many and the run is slow for no gain. It is one of the two settings (with the k-mesh) that you must test on every new system <Cite id="S-042" />.

</Why>

<Intuition>

Think of a photograph. A few big pixels give a blurry picture, many small pixels a sharp one. A plane wave is a pure ripple with one wavelength, and a function in a crystal can be built by adding ripples of different wavelengths. Long ripples give the smooth, large-scale shape. Short ripples add the fine detail.

The <Term id="cutoff-energy">cutoff energy</Term> says where to stop adding ripples. A short wavelength means a large kinetic energy, so the rule is: keep every plane wave whose kinetic energy is below `ENCUT`. Raise `ENCUT` and the set grows, like adding pixels <Cite id="S-049" />.

Three things follow, and the lab below shows them:

- **The set is a sphere.** Kinetic energy depends only on the length of the wave vector, so "below the cutoff" picks all the points inside a ball in reciprocal space.
- **The count grows quickly.** The ball's volume grows as the cube of its radius, and the radius grows as the square root of the energy, so the number of plane waves grows roughly as `ENCUT` to the power 3/2. Doubling `ENCUT` makes the set about 2.8 times bigger.
- **The energy settles.** As more plane waves are added, the total energy falls and flattens out. The toy plot at the bottom of the lab shows that.

Why this value: you want the smallest `ENCUT` after which the quantity you care about no longer changes noticeably. What happens if you skip the test: you may report an energy or a lattice constant that is a property of your basis set, not of your material.

<Level min="intermediate">

Hard parts of the wavefunction need the shortest waves. Near a nucleus the true orbitals wiggle rapidly, which is why a raw plane-wave basis would need a huge cutoff. VASP avoids that by replacing the core region with a smooth stand-in (the next topic), so the cutoff you need is set mostly by the hardest element in the cell, usually oxygen or a first-row atom <Cite id="S-019" />.

</Level>

</Intuition>

<Lab id="encut-sphere" title="The cutoff sphere" />

<TheMath>

A plane wave of wave vector $\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}$ has kinetic energy

$$E = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,|\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}|^2,$$

where $\mathbf{G}$ is a reciprocal lattice vector and $\hbar^2/2m = 3.80998$ eV Å$^2$ <Cite id="S-031" />. The basis keeps every $\mathbf{G}$ with $E \le E_{\text{cut}}$, that is, every $\mathbf{G}$ inside a sphere of radius

$$G_{\text{cut}} = \sqrt{E_{\text{cut}} \big/ (\hbar^2/2m)}.$$

Each lattice point owns a volume $(2\pi)^3/V$ of reciprocal space, with $V$ the cell volume, so the number of plane waves is about

$$N_{\text{pw}} \approx \frac{V\,G_{\text{cut}}^3}{6\pi^2} \;\propto\; V\,E_{\text{cut}}^{3/2}.$$

The lab counts the points exactly and compares them with this estimate. The exact count jumps in small steps, so the two agree well for large cells and high cutoffs and less well for tiny ones.

<Level min="pro">

Plane-wave energies converge variationally: more waves can only lower the total energy at fixed volume. The energy at fixed $E_{\text{cut}}$ changes when the cell changes, because the number of waves below the cutoff changes in steps. That mismatch is the origin of Pulay stress, discussed below <Cite id="S-040" />.

</Level>

</TheMath>

<InVasp>

- `ENCUT` sets the cutoff in eV. If you leave it out, VASP uses the largest `ENMAX` among the `POTCAR` files you use (for `PREC = Normal` or `Accurate`) <Cite id="S-019" />. `ENMAX` is a recommended minimum stored in each `POTCAR`. Read it with `grep ENMAX POTCAR`; this prints one number and never exposes the file's contents.
- The author's rule: for runs that change the cell (relaxations with `ISIF = 3`, elastic constants, equations of state), use `ENCUT = 1.3 × max(ENMAX)`. For other runs, at least `max(ENMAX)` <Cite id="S-041" /> <Cite id="S-042" />.
- **Pulay stress.** When the cell volume changes, the number of plane waves under a fixed cutoff changes too, which adds an artificial pressure. Stress converges much more slowly than energy, so cell relaxations need the extra 30 percent margin <Cite id="S-040" />.
- **Convergence test.** Run a series of static calculations with the same structure, same k-mesh and rising `ENCUT` (for example 400, 450, ..., 700 eV). Pick the smallest `ENCUT` at which the energy per atom changes by less than about 1 meV from the next step up. For stress-based quantities, watch the pressure as well <Cite id="S-042" />. The site's convergence tool plots such a series for you.
- Keep `ENCUT` identical in every calculation whose energies you compare.
- `PREC = High` sets the cutoff to 1.3 times `ENMAX` by default <Cite id="S-019" />.

</InVasp>

<WhatChanges>

| If you change...                               | ...the lab and the numbers                        | ...in VASP                                                       |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `ENCUT` up                                     | More plane waves (about `ENCUT` to the power 3/2) | Slower runs, more memory; energy flatter                         |
| `ENCUT` too low                                | The toy energy curve is still falling             | Wrong stress and lattice constants; energies off                 |
| A bigger cell at the same `ENCUT`              | More plane waves in proportion to the volume      | Same accuracy per atom, heavier run                              |
| Different `ENCUT` between two runs you compare | Not shown                                         | The energy difference mixes basis-set error with the real effect |

Why test: the cutoff that is enough depends on the atoms and on what you compute. Why 1.3 times `ENMAX` for relaxations: to keep Pulay stress small. What happens if you get it wrong: wrong cell sizes that look like physics.

</WhatChanges>

<Mistakes>

<Callout variant="mistake" title="Relaxing the cell at the default ENCUT">

The default is fine for a fixed-cell energy and often too low for a cell relaxation. Use 1.3 times `ENMAX` and re-run the relaxation from its own output.

</Callout>

<Callout variant="mistake" title="Comparing energies from different cutoffs">

Total energies are only comparable at the same `ENCUT`. Test convergence on differences, not on one absolute energy.

</Callout>

<Callout variant="mistake" title="Reading the toy curve as a real result">

The energy plot in the lab is a toy model to show the shape of a convergence test. It is not a prediction for any material.

</Callout>

</Mistakes>
